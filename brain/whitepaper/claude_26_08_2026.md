# s3ntiment — a protocol for honest feedback

**Draft, reconstructed from project history Sep 2025 – Jul 2026.**

---

## 0. What this document is, and what it is not

The **specs** (`brain/specs/`) describe the code as it is, package by package, for someone about to
change it. This document does the opposite job: it describes s3ntiment as a **protocol** — a set of
requirements that must hold, organised by function rather than by package — and shows the current
stack as *one reference implementation* of those requirements.

The distinction matters because every functional area below cuts across four or five packages.
"Invitation" alone lives in the contract, in `shared/invites`, in both frontends, and in the
Safe-signed batch derivation. Reading it as a protocol requirement is the only way to see it whole.

Each area states: **what must be true**, **how we make it true**, **what we rejected**, and **where
the implementation currently falls short**. That last part is deliberate — see §6.

---

## 1. The problem

Organisations make decisions based on what people *say* they want, rather than what they *actually*
want. Not because anyone is lying, but because the conditions for honest expression usually don't
exist.

Timur Kuran called this **preference falsification**: people publicly state preferences that differ
from their private ones, out of ordinary self-protection. You adjust what you say to what the room
will accept.

The concrete version: an organisation considers a significant change. The manager asks the team —
in a meeting, or via a survey that is *called* anonymous but where everyone privately wonders. Nine
say "good plan." Privately, eight of ten think it will fail. The decision is made on nine yeses. The
organisation stalls, and nobody understands why, because the data said there was support.

The data was accurate. It measured what people said. It just wasn't measuring what people thought.

**Every survey tool asks you to trust its privacy policy.** The gap between "we promise not to look"
and "we cannot look" is not a technicality — it is the difference between measuring speech and
measuring belief.

## 2. The evidence that this is real

The behavioural literature is unusually consistent here.

- **Social desirability bias** (Marlowe–Crowne, 1960s onward): respondents systematically skew
  answers toward what they believe the asker wants to hear, or what makes them look good.
- **Mode effects on sensitive topics** (sexual behaviour, drug use, income, voting): studies show
  **15–30% differences in reported rates** between face-to-face and anonymous equivalents. The
  numbers literally change when people feel unobserved.
- **Randomised response techniques**: reported rates of stigmatised behaviours (infidelity, drug
  use, tax evasion) rise **2–3×** compared with standard anonymous surveys — and the effect is
  strongest in **high-trust, high-accountability environments**, which is exactly where
  organisational surveys are run.
- **Espoused theory vs theory-in-use** (Argyris & Schön): people report what they believe they
  *should* believe, not what they act on. Goffman's dramaturgical framing is the same observation —
  people perform a version of themselves calibrated to an audience.

Two findings matter most for design:

1. **The gap between stated and revealed preference is widest exactly where the stakes are
   highest** — which is where surveys are most consequential and least reliable.
2. **Quality doesn't only improve on sensitive questions; it improves across the board**, because
   respondents relax their general vigilance once privacy is credible.

That last point is the thesis: **mathematical privacy doesn't just change what people say about
sensitive things, it changes how honestly they engage overall.**

The category this creates is **verified-honest data** as distinct from self-reported data. Not a
claim about who is lying — a claim about the conditions under which honest expression is possible at
all.

## 3. The four pillars

These are the yardstick. Every architectural decision in §5 is measured against them, and several
were rejected purely for failing one.

### Pillar 1 — Privacy by design
Deliberately borrowing GDPR Article 25's language, then meeting it architecturally rather than
contractually. Three layers:

1. **Your identity is yours alone** — no identifier is stored that could link a response to a person.
2. **Access is enforced by math, not people** — decryption is gated by cryptographic conditions, not
   by an access-control list somebody administers.
3. **Your data is never whole in one place** — *data minimisation as architecture, not as policy.*

### Pillar 2 — Data sovereignty (not a platform)
Infrastructure, not platform. Three positive framings rather than defining ourselves by a negation:

- **Infrastructure** — it runs on neutral networks, not our servers.
- **Replaceable** — open source; the organiser owns their keys and data.
- **No access** — there is no master key. We cannot read your data, and this is structural.

The operative check is the **walk-away test**: *can a pool take its keys and its data, stop using
s3ntiment entirely, and keep operating?* If a design makes the answer "no", it fails this pillar.

The regulatory hook is the EU's digital-sovereignty agenda. Most vendors answer data sovereignty
with "we host in the EU." s3ntiment answers it with **there is no central host** — no server to
subpoena, no jurisdiction problem, no single party holding the data.

### Pillar 3 — One invitation, one unique participation
Sybil resistance without identity. You can prove someone was invited and hasn't already participated
without knowing, or storing, who they are.

### Pillar 4 — Continuous feedback
Surveys that run on. Respondents revise answers, sentiment or status when their view changes; the
organiser reads a current result at any moment. A **barometer**, not a snapshot. Abstention and "no
opinion yet" are valid states, not missing data.

> **Note on ordering.** Two pillars are grounded in recognised regulatory concepts (for the
> compliance reader), two are UX propositions (for the people who actually use it). Lead with
> privacy — that's the compliance reader's pain point.

## 4. Core model

A **pool** is a named collection of surveys with a shared respondent registry — a panel.
Respondents join a pool once and can then answer any survey in it. **A standalone survey is a
degenerate case: a pool with exactly one survey. No special casing.**

An **invitation** admits someone to a *pool*, not to a survey. Once admitted, participation is
entirely off-chain and leaves no trace on any public ledger.

The privacy properties that follow:

- The chain records only *"this address is a member of this pool"* — one write, no survey detail.
- Survey participation is invisible on-chain.
- Cross-survey correlation **within** a pool is expected — that's what a panel is for.
- Cross-pool correlation is impossible by construction.

## 5. The protocol, by function

### 5.1 Admission — one invitation, one participation

**What must be true**
- Only invited people can participate.
- An invitation can be used exactly once, provably.
- Using an invitation reveals nothing about who used it.
- Issuing invitations must not require a wallet interaction per invitation.

**How we make it true**
The pool's Safe signs a single random seed, deriving an **ephemeral batch wallet**. Its address is
registered on-chain as the `batchId`. Every card's nullifier is then signed locally by that wallet —
no further prompts. Cards are printed as QR codes carrying `{ nullifier, batchId, signature,
poolId }`.

On redemption, the contract recovers the signer, checks it equals the registered batch wallet,
checks the nullifier is unused, burns it, and records membership. One transaction, ever.

**What we rejected**
- **Sign each card individually with the creator's wallet.** Maximum trustlessness; N wallet popups
  for N cards. Unusable.
- **Sign with a platform key.** Zero popups, but it makes s3ntiment the authority that decides who
  is invited. Fails Pillar 2 outright.
- **Separate batch UUIDs.** The signature recovery already yields the address — a second identifier
  is pure indirection. `batchId` *is* the wallet address.

**Accepted limitation, stated openly:** the contract cannot verify *how* a batch wallet was derived,
so it cannot prevent a pool owner minting extra cards. That is outside the threat model — the owner
already controls batch size. The threat model is **respondent-side forgery**, and that is fully
closed.

**Two invitation media, two kinds of cohort**
- A **physical card** handed out in a place produces a *time-and-place bound cohort* — "people who
  were at this exhibition." The physical distribution is itself the quality control, and the
  exclusivity is tangible.
- A **personal email invitation** produces a *criteria-bound cohort* — "members", "contributors",
  "past attendees." Lower friction, targeted, supports reminders and re-invitation, scales without
  logistics.

Both preserve the same property, and it's the one open-link survey tools cannot offer: **cohort
membership is provable but anonymous.**

### 5.2 Identity — durable access without an identifier

**What must be true**
- A respondent can return, months later, and reach their own data.
- Nothing stored anywhere can map that access back to a real-world identifier.
- No seed phrase.
- The identity used in one pool cannot be correlated with the identity used in another.

**How we make it true**
Authentication is email or phone via **WaaP (Human Wallet)**. The key is derived client-side from a
threshold OPRF evaluated across Human Network, combined with a security share held in a TEE
(2PC signing). Nobody custodies a private key.

Each pool registration mints a **fresh EOA** — the pool wallet — with no link to any prior identity.
The nilDB signer seed is derived deterministically from a signed message, so one login always
resolves to the same data identity, without that identity ever being stored against an email
address.

**What we rejected**
- **Personal-data nullifiers via Human Network VOPRF.** The original Oct 2025 design asked every
  respondent for name, date of birth and city of birth, and derived a sybil-resistance nullifier
  from them. Abandoned: it collected personal data *in order to prove someone hadn't participated* —
  the exact inversion of Pillar 1. Sybil resistance now comes from invitation scarcity plus on-chain
  burn (§5.1), not from identity.
- **Deferring login until after the first survey**, deriving the first-visit key from the card
  nullifier. Attractive on funnel grounds; rejected because the nullifier is already the
  participation proof — reusing it as a key seed turns a photographed card into a stolen signing
  key. The card→login binding also has to live somewhere, and every location leaks.
- **Fireblocks** (custodial-flavoured, wrong story for Pillar 2) and **Privy** (battle-tested, but
  acquired by Stripe — same "trust a company" profile).

**Honest trade-off, on the record:** the OPRF input is a low-entropy identifier. Security rests on
the Human Network threshold holding — compromise enough nodes and every email's share becomes
derivable — plus the TEE-held security share. This is materially weaker than a high-entropy seed
phrase. It is a deliberate trade of entropy for the ability to onboard people who will never manage
a seed phrase, and it should be described that way rather than glossed.

### 5.3 Access — only members can read the survey

**What must be true**
- Only registered pool members can decrypt a survey.
- Only the pool's governing signers can decrypt the privileged variant (the one containing scoring).
- The check must be enforced cryptographically, not by an application deciding to enforce it.
- s3ntiment must not be able to bypass it.

**How we make it true**
Each survey config is encrypted twice with different content: an **owner variant** (with scoring) and
a **respondent variant** (scoring stripped). Decryption runs inside a Lit Action executing in a
hardware-isolated TEE, which reads the chain directly and refuses unless:

- *respondent path* — `isPoolMember(poolId, userAddress)`; or
- *owner path* — `isPoolSafe(safe, poolId)` **and** `isOwner(userAddress)` on that Safe.

The pool's constants are compiled into the action source, so the action's IPFS CID *is* the
authorisation: a pool's group can only run the actions built for that pool.

**What we rejected / what changed under us**
- **Access Control Conditions** (the previous Lit generation) were the mechanism until Lit replaced
  threshold cryptography with TEE execution. Conditions are now code. **This shifts responsibility
  onto us**: a malformed condition used to fail closed at the network layer; a missing guard clause
  now simply decrypts. Access checks are security code, reviewed as such.
- **Passing `poolId` as a runtime parameter** — rejected. Any holder of a usage key could then
  point an action at another pool's condition.
- **A single s3ntiment-controlled key group containing every pool's key.** Trivial onboarding; makes
  s3ntiment the trust anchor, the governance bottleneck and the single point of regulatory pressure.
  Killed by the walk-away test.

### 5.4 Respondent data — owned, editable, erasable

**What must be true**
- The respondent owns their record.
- They can revise it whenever their view changes (Pillar 4) and delete it entirely.
- The organiser never sees an individual response.
- s3ntiment cannot read it either.
- If s3ntiment disappears, the respondent's data and the organiser's results survive.

**How we make it true (design)**
Responses live in Nillion nilDB. The record's owner is the respondent's own DID; the ACL grants the
survey owner `read` and `execute` but never `write`. The builder key pays for storage and mints
delegations but is never named as grantee — so "we pay for the infrastructure and cryptographically
cannot read your answers" is structural rather than promised. Revision is delete-then-recreate,
which is also what makes continuous feedback work: resubmission isn't a blocked duplicate, it's the
intended path.

**What we rejected**
- **Delegation without ownership.** If the platform owns the collection and the organiser's access
  is a delegation token, then when the platform disappears the token expires and the organiser is
  permanently locked out of their own results. Delegation is a convenience layer, never a
  sovereignty mechanism — it fails the walk-away test outright. The resolution: the collection is
  owned by the survey owner, whose data key is encrypted to them so they can recover it and pay for
  their own storage if they ever need to.

**⚠ Where the implementation currently falls short — the honest section**

This is the largest gap between this document and the running code, and it is deliberate rather than
accidental:

- **Owned collections do not yet work.** Nillion's own guidance is to use standard collections for
  full-stack applications; owned collections aren't fully supported. Every attempt hit SDK routing
  and node-side validation failures.
- **Consequence:** the live write path has the respondent POST to our backend, and the *builder*
  writes the record into a *builder-owned* collection. The owned-collection code path exists, is
  correct, and is deliberately retained for the day this can be reversed.
- **What is true today:** respondents can edit and delete their answers, and the organiser only ever
  receives aggregates. That is honoured *operationally* — by the API — but not yet *cryptographically*.
  Pillar 1's third layer is correspondingly weakened.

Stating this plainly is the point. A protocol document that describes the intended cryptography as
though it were shipped would be the same "trust our promise" move the whole project exists to
replace.

### 5.5 Aggregation — results without exposure

**What must be true**
- The organiser sees tallies, never individual responses.
- The organiser's access to their own results does not depend on s3ntiment's continued existence.
- Where surveys are scored, the answer key must not leak to respondents.
- Results must be readable at any moment (Pillar 4), not only after the survey closes.

**How we make it true**
Aggregation runs against the survey's collection and returns tallies per question type. The
organiser's read access is a long-lived delegation scoped by policy to exactly one collection. For
scored surveys, `stripScoring()` separates the answer key from the respondent-facing config so that
the key never travels on the path respondents can decrypt.

**The scoring dilemma — and an explicitly temporary answer**

Who may decrypt correct answers and compute scores? The tension is **immediacy versus integrity**: an
ad hoc quiz wants a score the instant you submit; an exam must stop early finishers leaking answers
to those still answering. Four options:

1. **Completion-gated** — an on-chain completion flag unlocks decryption. Clean, but leaky: a
   finished participant can simply tell someone who hasn't submitted.
2. **Time-gated** — answers unlock at a fixed time. Works for exams, breaks ad hoc use; you can't
   know in advance when the last person submits.
3. **Owner-gated** — the organiser closes the survey manually. Same ad hoc problem, plus a human
   step that can be forgotten or manipulated.
4. **Quizmaster-gated** — the key is encrypted to a trusted third party.

We chose (4), with the platform's own data identity as the quizmaster: the scoring object is
encrypted to the builder DID with ECIES and decrypted server-side. It works for every quiz type, with
no timing constraints and no contract changes.

**⚠ The cost:** s3ntiment holds that key. We *could* decrypt any answer key; a server compromise
exposes all of them. **This contradicts Pillar 2.** It is marked as a stepping stone in the code and
here.

**A correction worth preserving:** moving this into a confidential-compute enclave was considered and
is *not* sufficient. An enclave stops us reading plaintext during computation, but we still choose
which program runs and still hold the identity that gates decryption — the platform risk moves
rather than reduces. What actually satisfies Pillar 2 is either (a) a key held by a threshold keypair
released only by an immutable, auditable action, or (b) encrypting the answer key to the pool's own
Safe, making the organiser sovereign and s3ntiment a conduit. (a) matches the rest of the
architecture and is the intended direction.

### 5.6 Governance — collective, with no privileged organiser

**What must be true**
- A pool can be governed by several people together.
- No organiser holds more rights than any other.
- s3ntiment holds no administrative override.
- Governance survives any individual leaving.

**How we make it true**
The pool owner is a **Safe multisig**. Pool and survey mutations are executed by that Safe. There is
no s3ntiment-side organiser account with elevated rights, and no owner field that could be
transferred by us. The Safe's signer set *is* the governance model — adding or removing an organiser
is a Safe operation, not a support request.

The same Safe is the intended owner of the pool's encryption infrastructure (§5.3), which is what
converts "we cannot decrypt your data" from a policy into a fact about which keys exist and who
holds them.

**⚠ Current status:** the encryption infrastructure is presently provisioned under an s3ntiment-held
account — the SaaS posture — with per-pool isolation but not yet per-pool ownership. The migration
path is real (hand the account to the pool's Safe; there are no "modes", only who owns what) but has
not been walked. Until it is, the walk-away test passes on code and data but not on keys.

**Open design question:** whether an individual Safe signer may create a survey directly, or whether
every survey requires a full multisig transaction. The original design had the asymmetry — pool
creation is governance, survey creation is operational — but the deployed contract enforces the
stricter rule. This is the single biggest open question for organiser experience.

## 6. Honest caveats

Kept deliberately. The judgment throughout this project has been that transparency about limitations
builds more credibility than a clean pitch — and being caught overselling would destroy exactly the
thing being sold.

1. **The walk-away test passes on code, not yet on infrastructure.** The code is open source and a
   pool owns its data. But frontends need re-hosting, the backend needs redeploying, and **Nillion is
   a company that could revoke API access**. "No central host" is true of the data layer and not yet
   true of the operational layer.
2. **Key ownership has not migrated to pools.** §5.6. Per-pool isolation today; per-pool sovereignty
   is a target.
3. **Respondent data ownership is operational, not yet cryptographic.** §5.4.
4. **We hold scoring answer keys.** §5.5.
5. **Identity security rests on a threshold network with low-entropy inputs.** §5.2.
6. **Cross-survey correlation within a pool is possible and intended.** This is the panel model
   working as designed, not a leak — but it should be said out loud, because a respondent should
   know that answering three surveys in one pool is linkable *within* that pool.

Items 2–4 compound. Together they mean that today the strongest honest claim is *"the architecture
is built so that we cannot see your data, and three specific pieces of that architecture are not yet
switched on."* That is a much better sentence than a false absolute, and it is falsifiable, which is
the point.

## 7. Reference implementation

The protocol above is stack-independent. This is one implementation of it.

| Function | Requirement | Current choice |
|---|---|---|
| Admission | one-time, unlinkable invitation | Ephemeral batch wallet + on-chain nullifier burn (Base) |
| Identity | durable access, no stored identifier | WaaP / Human Wallet (threshold OPRF + TEE 2PC) |
| Access | cryptographic gating on membership | Lit Protocol Actions in TEE, per-pool keys and action CIDs |
| Respondent data | user-owned, editable, erasable | Nillion nilDB + NUC delegation |
| Aggregation | tallies only, owner-recoverable | nilDB queries + policy-scoped read delegation |
| Governance | collective, no privileged party | Safe multisig as pool owner |
| Config storage | content-addressed, immutable | IPFS (Pinata / Kubo) |
| Gas abstraction | respondent needs no funds | Smart contract account + paymaster |

Anything meeting the §5 requirements is a valid s3ntiment implementation. The stack is chosen, not
essential — and two of these components have already been replaced once (see §5.2, §5.3), which is
the argument for writing the requirements down separately from the choices.

## 7A. What this is for: a protocol for panels

The six requirements in §5 describe a survey system. But the thing they compose into is more
specific than that, and worth naming: **infrastructure for running a respondent panel.**

### A pool is a panel

Recall the core model (§4): respondents join a pool once, then answer any survey in it, revising
their answers as their views change, under governance shared by the pool's organisers. That is the
definition of a panel — a standing body of respondents, tracked over time, rather than an audience
assembled fresh for each questionnaire.

This reframes one item from §6. Cross-survey correlation within a pool was listed there as a
property requiring disclosure. In panel terms it is the central capability: linking a respondent's
answers across surveys over time is exactly what a panel is *for*, and what distinguishes
longitudinal research from a series of disconnected polls. Cross-pool correlation remaining
impossible is what keeps that acceptable. The property is unchanged; the framing is more honest
about why it exists.

### What a panel operator needs, and what the incumbent model costs

The dominant model is well understood. A panel company recruits members through advertising and
partnerships, collects demographics at signup, invites a matched sub-sample for each study, pays
participants in points, and weights the results to correct residual skew against the target
population. It works, and it is expensive — the recruitment and maintenance of the panel is
generally worth more than the survey software running on top of it.

It also carries three costs that the incumbents cannot design away:

1. **The panelist is the product.** Members hand their answers to a company that owns them, sells
   access to them, and may be acquired.
2. **Sybil resistance requires identification.** Preventing duplicate participation is done by
   knowing who people are — mainstream tools log IP addresses explicitly for this purpose.
   Anonymity and fraud prevention are in direct tension.
3. **Fraud is an arms race being lost.** Professional respondents, survey farms, and now
   machine-generated open-ended answers are met with screening heuristics and detection models,
   which is a losing position by construction.

s3ntiment's answer to (1) is respondent-owned records; to (2) invitation scarcity plus a one-time
on-chain burn, which achieves the same guarantee at the opposite privacy cost; and to (3) the
observation that the property is **provable to the research client**, not merely asserted by the
panel provider. A buyer can verify the sybil resistance rather than trusting a QA claim.

### Who runs a pool

The protocol does not solve recruitment, and it is not intended to. It is aimed at organisations
that **already hold a cohort and lack a way to run a panel on it**: a trade association with member
firms, a union with members, a city with residents, a media outlet with subscribers, a professional
body with practitioners, a collective of researchers sharing a sample frame, a distributed
contributor community. An employer running a continuous employee barometer is doing the same thing
under a different name.

These operators need no acquisition budget — they have the cohort. What they lack is infrastructure
their own members will trust with individual answers. A union surveying its members through a
conventional platform has a credibility problem that no privacy policy resolves, and the resulting
data carries exactly the distortion described in §1 and §2.

This is also where §5.6 stops being abstract. Collective governance with no privileged organiser is
not a philosophical preference when the panel belongs to its members; it is a functional
requirement.

### The open problem: sampling without identification

The incumbent method depends on knowing who panelists are. s3ntiment deliberately stores nothing.
This is a real and unresolved tension, and it should be stated as such rather than finessed.

A route exists that is consistent with the rest of the architecture: collect demographic attributes
as encrypted answers *within* the pool rather than as registration metadata; compute weighting in
aggregate, since "of respondents reporting age 25–34, X% answered Y" requires no identification; and
handle targeted invitation with an access condition of the form `isPoolMember AND matches criteria`,
evaluated inside the same trusted execution described in §5.3, so a respondent proves eligibility
without the operator learning who they are.

What that route does not yet solve:

- **Quota sampling.** You cannot chase a quota you cannot observe filling.
- **Non-response bias.** If a subgroup systematically declines, its absence is invisible.
- **Weighting validity.** Aggregate knowledge of the achieved sample may or may not support
  defensible weights; this needs a survey methodologist, not an engineer.
- **Self-reported attributes** cannot be validated against an external record.

The honest conclusion is that s3ntiment may not serve general-population representative research,
and may not need to. Panels drawn from **known cohorts** — members, employees, residents,
contributors — have a defined universe, where the composition of the population is known even when
the identity of respondents is not. That is a materially easier problem, and it covers every
operator described above.

### What this framing demands that isn't built yet

Two things in §6 change weight under this reading. **Respondent compensation** stops being optional:
panels run on incentives, and while paying anonymous accounts is one area where this architecture is
straightforwardly better than gift cards and payment processors, it is not yet implemented. And the
shortfall in §5.4 — respondent data ownership being operational rather than cryptographic — stops
being a gap between architecture and aspiration. If the claim is that panelists are not the product,
then the fact that individual records are currently written and held by the platform contradicts the
claim directly. It is the first thing to fix.

## 8. Where this goes

The panel protocol (§7A) proves the privacy technology works and builds the trust needed for the
harder product behind it: **mediation**. The dependency runs one way — mediation needs a standing
body of participants who already trust the privacy guarantees, which is what a panel is.

The mediation landscape splits into two inadequate camps. Traditional AI mediation tools offer
conflict resolution without cryptographic privacy — trust-based confidentiality, and no way to handle
pseudonymous participants. Blockchain arbitration platforms (Kleros, Aragon Court) are decentralised
but fundamentally *adversarial*: they produce winners and losers by jury vote, evidence is public, and
there is no synthesis toward common ground.

s3ntiment sits in neither camp. If Kleros is a decentralised court, this is closer to decentralised
facilitation.

**Four kinds of mediation outcome:**

1. **Win-win expansion** (1+1=3) — find a creative option neither side considered.
2. **Latent consensus discovery** — *find the 2 we already agreed on*. Everyone is arguing about the
   surface while aligned on fundamentals.
3. **Fair compromise** — split the difference where disagreement is genuine.
4. **Process clarification** — discover there was no conflict, only miscommunication.

**Privacy matters most for (2)**, and that is the differentiator. In public forums people defend
positions they don't fully hold, because reputation is on the line and compromise reads as weakness.
Given a private channel, synthesis across submissions can reveal alignment that could never surface
publicly — and reveal it *without exposing who said what*, so people can update their position
without losing face.

A worked example: three contributors argue publicly for "aggressive growth", "sustainable building"
and "community focus" — apparently irreconcilable. Privately, each qualifies their position: *growth,
but not at the cost of quality; sustainability, but with enough traction to survive; community, but
resourced.* The synthesis is that all three want measured growth that strengthens the community. Not
1+1=3 — revealing the 2 that was there all along, hidden under different framings.

**And when there is no consensus**, the honest outcome is knowing that. Mapping the fault lines
clearly — "we disagree on strategy but agree on values, so run separate workstreams" — is a real
result.

So the claim to make is not *"we always find consensus"*, which is false. It is:

> We reveal what people actually think, stripped of posturing and reputation games. Sometimes that's
> hidden consensus. Sometimes it's irreconcilable difference. Either way, you'll know.

This capability is architecturally prepared for and deliberately parked. Private inference over
responses is orthogonal to getting storage and access control right, and doing it before the
foundations are sound would mean synthesising data we shouldn't yet be able to read.

## 9. Summary

An organisation that only promises anonymity measures what people say. s3ntiment is built to measure
what people think — and to be checkable on that claim rather than believed on it.

The protocol requires six things: unlinkable one-time admission; durable identity with no stored
identifier; cryptographically gated access; respondent-owned data; aggregate-only results; and
collective governance with no privileged party. The current stack implements all six, three of them
incompletely, and §6 says which three.

Composed together, those six requirements are infrastructure for a respondent panel (§7A) — a
standing body of people who can be surveyed repeatedly over time without any of them becoming
identifiable, owned, or counted twice. That is the near-term product. Mediation (§8) is what the
same guarantees make possible once a panel exists and trusts them.

The specs say what the code does. This document says what has to be true, and where we aren't there
yet.
